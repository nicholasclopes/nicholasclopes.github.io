Supercritical Fluids

My work with supercritical fluids (SCFs) focused on understanding the heat transfer mechanisms associated with variable property flows and improving how we model them. We used CFD to look at a range of factors that can affect SCF cooling, including thermal boundary conditions, inlet conditions, flow development, channel geometry, buoyancy, and surface roughness. We employed a range of modeling approaches, including correlation-based methods, 2D and 3D CFD simulations, and machine-learning models. These projects have helped us better understand both the underlying physics of SCF heat transfer and some of the challenges involved in modeling these flow accurately while balancing engineering practicality.

A3 Refrigerant Safety

As part of a team with Oak Ridge National Laboratory, we developed CFD models to study how refrigerants released from HVAC equipment disperse through mechanical rooms. We first used carbon dioxide to develop and validate a unique CFD modeling approach, in which we employed a two-stage method that captured both the small-scale, highly transient leak and the large-scale, nearly quiescent flow in the room. We then applied this approach to propane, an A3 refrigerant, to examine how real leaks could spread through a mechanical room and where concentrations could become relevant to lower-flammability-limit (LFL) and ignition considerations. This work allowed us to better understand refrigerant safety and potential mitigation strategies for HVAC systems.

Combustor Cooling

My work at the German Aerospace Center (DLR) explored cooling techniques for gas-turbine combustors. We investigated a novel effusion hole design and looked at how certain geometric features affected the cooling jet physics and hot-side surface temperatures. The work also showed how more complex cooling geometries, made possible by modern manufacturing techniques, could provide new options for improving combustor thermal management.
